MERLIN BIRD BUDDIES

The whole story: research, iterations and all.The short version, in five stops.

OVERVIEW

Merlin Bird Buddies reimagines the Merlin Bird ID app for kids ages 8 to 13, putting approachability and learning first. A softer color palette, illustrated moments and a gentler interface turn a precise tool for birders into a welcoming place to start.

Project
Merlin Bird Buddies
Type
UI / UX / Mobile App Redesign
Role
Solo: Research, UX/UI, Illustration
Tools
Figma, Procreate
Timeline
2-week class project, then extended on my own

THE CHALLENGE

The brief: reskin an existing app for a new audience. I chose Merlin Bird ID, the Cornell Lab of Ornithology’s bird identification app. I love it, but it’s built for adult birders, and it feels sterile and a little unwelcoming. I kept thinking about how much my younger cousin would enjoy it if it felt made for kids, so I set out to make it fun and educational for them.

APPROACH

Problem
Merlin Bird ID is a great tool, but it’s built for adult birders: sterile, precise and unwelcoming to kids.
Audience
Kids ages 8 to 13, like Maya, 9, who loves exploring her backyard but gets overwhelmed by busy screens.

THE ORIGINAL APP

I started by marking up the current app: what to keep, and what to rethink for kids. Tap a pin to read the note.

  1. Blank white space, with the logo placed awkwardly at the very top.

  2. The bird of the day photo fills most of the screen. Could it be smaller, illustrated, maybe even interactive?

  3. The large buttons work, but the composition feels disjointed and segmented.

  4. The navigation icons could be more fun and playful for a younger audience.

  1. “Life List” is a bit boring, and could be confusing for kids. Ideas: My Birds, My Sightings, Bird Book, Flight Log, Pecking Order.

  2. The toolbar could be simpler. Does it need the info button?

  3. The information gets dense in that little space: name, place and date are all packed into each row.

  1. The spectrogram is precise but very scientific, and a little dull for kids. It needs color and simplicity.

  2. The suggestion list works really well, with a good balance between text and image. Worth keeping.

  3. The recording bar sits right on top of the navigation: two rows of buttons stacked together. Could that transition be easier?

RESEARCH

I explored two directions, each written for a different kid. Storybook Style was bright and game-like, for Henry, 6. Tranquil Learning was calm and artistic, for Maya, 9. I moved forward with Maya. Identifying birds by sound and photo suits slightly older kids, and a quieter app felt truer to birding and kinder to kids who get overwhelmed easily.

DESIGN DECISIONS

  1. 01

    Calm, not loud

    Maya gets overwhelmed by things that are too colorful or busy. A soft palette of cream, muted purple and sage keeps the app gentle, with one bright red saved for the record button.

  2. 02

    An illustrated welcome

    Instead of a photo filling the screen, the bird of the day is my own illustration, drawn in Procreate and set in a landscape. It makes the first screen feel like a picture book.

  3. 03

    Words a kid would use

    “Life List” became “Flight Log,” the identify buttons got short labels (Steps, Sound, Image), and the spectrogram became a simple sound wave.

  4. 04

    Real birds stay real

    Bird photos stay photos, so what kids learn matches what they see outside. Explore turns it into a challenge: can you find these birds nearby?

WHAT’S NEXT

  1. 01

    Badges to collect

    Unlock badges for spotting birds from different regions, seasons or families, like warblers or woodpeckers.

  2. 02

    Mystery bird of the day

    A daily quiz with a silhouette, call or close-up detail, multiple choice answers and playful hints.

  3. 03

    Make it theirs

    Let kids pick favorite birds and add their own notes and drawings to the Flight Log.

  4. 04

    A working prototype

    An interactive prototype, to test the flow with kids and show the transitions between screens.

REFLECTION

I’ve worked several jobs in childcare and have several younger cousins, so designing for kids felt natural, and it was so much fun to design for an audience that really appreciates whimsy. The project also pulled me into a style I don’t usually work in, so I experimented a lot with how to convey my ideas. What helped most was how much brainstorming I did before production, which is unusual for me, and it taught me how valuable that early definition is. I kept going after the two-week brief because I wanted Bird Buddies to feel like a real app, not just two screens.
